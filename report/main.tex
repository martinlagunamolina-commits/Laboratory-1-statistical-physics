\textbf{Abstract}\par
\begin{minipage}{0.88\textwidth}
Independent unbiased coins provide an exactly solvable two-state model.
We enumerate the 128 microstates of seven coins, compute exact multiplicities
and log-space distributions, and measure computational scaling. We derive
finite-system probabilities, Boltzmann entropy changes and waiting times, then
distinguish concentration of the heads fraction from the decreasing probability
of any single macrostate in the thermodynamic limit. Automated checks compare
the implementation with independent exact combinatorial values. The group suffixes give $M=31.20656885$; nearest-integer rounding gives $N=131$.
\end{minipage}

\section{Introduction \& Objectives}
The objective is to connect microstate counting with macrostate probabilities,
Boltzmann entropy and the thermodynamic limit. We enumerate a small system,
measure computational scaling, and calculate the group's probabilities,
entropy differences and waiting times.

Each coin is independent with $P(H)=P(T)=1/2$. A microstate specifies the
ordered outcomes of all $N$ coins; the macrostate $K=k$ specifies only the heads
count. Thus there are $2^N$ equiprobable microstates and $N+1$ macrostates:
\begin{equation}
\Om(N,k)=\binom Nk=\frac{N!}{k!(N-k)!},\qquad
P_k=2^{-N}\Om(N,k),\qquad \sum_{k=0}^N\Om(N,k)=2^N.
\label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
These are discrete sums, not exact integrals of a smoothed curve.

\subsection{Full Enumeration Wall (1.b)} Integer $s$ encodes an outcome through its
$N$ bits. The generator visits $s=0,\ldots,(1\ll N)-1$ and yields one integer at
a time. The timing workload consumes every yielded configuration and counts
it, without storing, formatting or printing the entire list. It therefore
measures full integer enumeration, not text-file writing or classification by heads.
Formatting all outcomes additionally costs $O(N2^N)$ character operations.
The enumeration loop requires $O(2^N)$ iterations and $O(N)$ live bits; calling
\texttt{list()} would instead require exponentially increasing memory.
Python's arbitrary-precision integers avoid fixed-width overflow but do not remove this cost.

\subsection{Practical Distribution Limit (1.d)} Full enumeration is unnecessary:
\begin{equation}
\ln\Om(N,k)=\ln\Gamma(N+1)-\ln\Gamma(k+1)-\ln\Gamma(N-k+1).
\label{eq:gamma}
\end{equation}
Logarithms prevent coefficient overflow, although subtracting large log-gamma
values can lose precision. Binary64 exponentiation overflows near $\ln\Om>709.78$,
reached by central coefficients at approximately $N=1030$.
Two retained float64 arrays require $16(N+1)$ bytes; plotting and temporaries
need additional memory. The full-grid algorithm costs $O(N)$ time and memory.
We measure calculation and vector-PDF plotting with a chosen 30-s timeout and
256-MiB peak-RSS acceptance budget, checked after execution rather than enforced
as an operating-system memory cap.
\begin{table}[H]\centering\scriptsize\caption{Full-grid distribution calculation and vector-PDF plotting.}\label{tab:distribution}\input{distribution_benchmark.tex}\end{table}
This is the maximum among the tested candidates under declared resource
budgets, not an exhaustive or hardware-independent maximum. Sparse evaluation
and analytic approximations permit much larger system sizes.

\subsection{Thermodynamic Limit (1.e)} $\E K=N/2$, $\operatorname{Var}K=N/4$,
$\sigma_K=\sqrt N/2$. For the heads fraction $X=K/N$,
$\sigma_X=1/(2\sqrt N)$ and $\sigma_K/\E K=N^{-1/2}$.
At $N=10^{23}$, $\sigma_K=1.58114\times10^{11}$ coins but
$\sigma_X=1.58114\times10^{-12}$. The fraction is sharply concentrated at $1/2$;
the absolute width in coins increases. Chebyshev gives
$P(|X-1/2|\geq\epsilon)\leq1/(4N\epsilon^2)$ for fixed $\epsilon>0$.

\subsection{Probability and Entropy Analysis (2.a--g)}
\textbf{2.a--d: Probabilities.} Equation~\eqref{eq:model} supplies the point
probabilities. For an inclusive interval,
\begin{equation}
P(k_1\leq K\leq k_2)=\sum_{k=k_1}^{k_2}
\exp[\ln\Om(N,k)-N\ln2].
\end{equation}
The implementation uses log-sum-exp for the summation. If the number of tails
$L=N-K$ is between $N/2-20$ and $N/2+20$, the heads count lies in the same
symmetric interval. This is why 2.d can use the heads distribution.

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}\caption{Group probabilities, entropy differences, waiting time and likelihood ratios for $N=131$.}\label{tab:group}\input{group_results.tex}\end{table}
All probabilities and $\Delta S/\kb$ are dimensionless. Waiting times are in
seconds. The numerical table is generated from \texttt{group\_results.json};
the group uses $N=131$, $k_0=65$, interval $56\leq K\leq75$ in 2.c, and $46\leq K\leq85$ in 2.d.

\textbf{2.e--g: Entropy and its sign.} Boltzmann's macrostate entropy is
\begin{equation}
S(k)=\kb\ln\Om(N,k),\qquad
\Delta S_{k_1k_2}=S(k_1)-S(k_2)
=\kb\ln\frac{\Om(N,k_1)}{\Om(N,k_2)},
\end{equation}
where $\kb=1.380649\times10^{-23}\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$ exactly in SI.
Therefore 2.e and 2.f are negative: offset states have fewer microstates than
the mode. Part 2.g is positive because the mode has more microstates than
$k=20$. The signs follow the manual's definition, which is the reverse of
``final minus initial'' if $k_1\to k_2$ is read as a temporal transition.
The labels ``high'' or ``moderate disorder'' are qualitative; the actual
ordering follows the computed multiplicities.

\subsection{Waiting Times and Likelihood (2.h--i)}
\textbf{2.h: Eight heads.} At $N$ individual coins per second, one independent
experiment tossing all $N$ coins takes one second. For success probability
$p_8=\binom N8 2^{-N}$ and independent complete trials, the first-success count
$T$ is geometric on $1,2,\ldots$:
\begin{equation}
P(T=t)=(1-p_8)^{t-1}p_8,\qquad
\E T=\frac1{p_8}\ \mathrm{s},\qquad
\sigma_T=\frac{\sqrt{1-p_8}}{p_8}\ \mathrm{s}.
\end{equation}
Thus a huge mean waiting time is not a deterministic deadline. For rare events
the standard deviation is approximately the mean. After $t$ complete trials,
$P(T\leq t)=1-(1-p_8)^t$. At a rate $f$ complete trials/s, both the mean and
standard deviation in seconds are divided by $f$. If ``$N$ coins/s'' instead
means sequentially replacing single coins, trials are correlated and this
geometric model is not justified without further dynamics.
The program retains $\log_{10}\E T$ to avoid overflow and also exports years,
using $365.25\times86400$ s/year as the conversion convention.

\textbf{2.i: Likelihood ratio.} The printed manual says $M\pm10$ heads, not
$N/2\pm10$. We evaluate the literal two cases:
\begin{equation}
R_\pm=\frac{P(K=k_0)}{P(K=q_\pm)}
=\exp[\ln\Om(N,k_0)-\ln\Om(N,q_\pm)],\qquad
q_\pm=\operatorname{round}_{\mathrm{half\mbox{-}up}}(M\pm10).
\end{equation}
For this group, $q_-=21$ and $q_+=41$. The same nearest-integer rounding
convention is used for these states. Both ratios exceed one because $k_0$ is a mode.

\subsection{Second Law of Thermodynamics Interpretation (2.j)}
\textbf{2.j: Statistical Second Law.} For finite $N$, a lower-multiplicity
macrostate has nonzero probability. Rare entropy decreases therefore do not
contradict the statistical interpretation of the Second Law. They are
overwhelmingly unlikely for a macroscopic entropy decrease, not forbidden by
the counting model. Macrostates are not equiprobable: microstates are.

Coin tossing also involves an external drive. This counting experiment alone
does not specify an isolated system's dynamics, energy exchanges or total
entropy production, so it cannot establish a deterministic entropy-increase
trajectory. It shows why equilibrium macrostates dominate when microstates
are sampled without bias. Individual ordered outcomes remain possible even
though almost every observed macroscopic fraction lies very near one half.
The maximal Boltzmann macrostate entropy must also be distinguished from the
Shannon/Gibbs entropy $N\kb\ln2$ of the uniform ensemble over all microstates.

\textbf{Numerical reliability.} Exact integer checks establish multiplicities
without sampling error. Floating-point comparisons use stated tolerances
in the tests, not statistical confidence intervals. Six-significant-figure
display is a presentation choice. Benchmarks are subject to host load and
are reported as observations; repeated measurements on the target laptop
are required to estimate their variability.

\subsection{Macroscopic Re-evaluation (2.k)}
For even $N$ and $k=N/2+\delta$ near the center, Stirling's formula yields
\begin{equation}
P_k\simeq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi N}}e^{-2\delta^2/N},\quad
\ln\Om(N,k)\simeq N\ln2-\tfrac12\ln(\pi N/2)-2\delta^2/N.
\label{eq:local}
\end{equation}
This local approximation is not appropriate for fixed small $k$ near an edge.
There, for fixed $m$, $\binom Nm\sim N^m/m!$. For fixed fraction $x=k/N$ in
$(0,1)$,
\begin{equation}
\ln\Om(N,Nx)\simeq N h(x)-\tfrac12\ln[2\pi N x(1-x)],\quad
h(x)=-x\ln x-(1-x)\ln(1-x).
\end{equation}
Hence $P(K\simeq Nx)$ is exponentially suppressed away from $x=1/2$, with
rate $\ln2-h(x)>0$. This gives the following separate answers to 2.a--j.
\begin{table}[H]\centering\small\caption{Macroscopic limits of the finite-system quantities.}\label{tab:limits}
\begin{tabular}{lp{0.83\linewidth}}\toprule
Part & Limit as $N\to\infty$ (fixed integer offsets)\\\midrule
2.a & $P_{30}\sim N^{30}2^{-N}/30!\to0$; use its logarithm for numerical representation.\\
2.b & $P_{N/2}\sim\sqrt{2/(\pi N)}\to0$, despite remaining a most probable single macrostate.\\
2.c & $P(|K-N/2|\leq10)\sim21\sqrt{2/(\pi N)}\to0$ for even $N$.\\
2.d & The symmetric tails interval has probability $\sim41\sqrt{2/(\pi N)}\to0$.\\
2.e & $\Delta S/\kb\sim-2(25)^2/N=-1250/N\to0^-$.\\
2.f & $\Delta S/\kb\sim-2(10)^2/N=-200/N\to0^-$.\\
2.g & $\Delta S/\kb\simeq N\ln2-\tfrac12\ln(\pi N/2)-20\ln N+\ln(20!)\to+\infty$.\\
2.h & $\E T\sim8!\,2^N/N^8$ s $\to\infty$; $\sigma_T/\E T\to1$.\\
2.i & For fixed valid $q_\pm=M\pm10=m$, $R_\pm\sim m!\,2^N\sqrt{2/(\pi N)}/N^m\to\infty$.\\
2.j & A fixed fractional imbalance becomes exponentially improbable. Finite-system fluctuations remain consistent with a statistical Second Law.\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}
For odd $N$, the literal intervals in 2.c--d contain 20 and 40 states instead
of 21 and 41. At $N=10^{23}$ (even), $P_{N/2}\simeq2.52313\times10^{-12}$;
the two fixed-width interval probabilities are approximately
$5.29858\times10^{-11}$ and $1.03448\times10^{-10}$.
Part 2.e gives $\Delta S\simeq-1.72581\times10^{-43}$ J/K, and 2.f gives
$-2.76130\times10^{-44}$ J/K. Part 2.g has leading value
$\Delta S\simeq0.956993$ J/K. For 2.h,
$\log_{10}(\E T/\mathrm{s})\simeq N\log_{10}2-8\log_{10}N+\log_{10}(8!)$,
whose leading term is $3.01030\times10^{22}$.

In this extrapolation, $N$ varies independently of the finite-group
assignment $100+M$, while the group's $M$ and rounded $q_\pm$ remain fixed.

Fixed-width intervals shrink relative to $\sigma_K$; this explains their
vanishing probability. An interval of fixed width in \emph{fraction} $K/N$
has probability tending to one. Direct log-gamma subtraction at $10^{23}$
cannot resolve small offsets in binary64; equations above are used analytically.

\section{Conclusion}
The binomial model connects exact microstate counting with macrostate
probabilities and Boltzmann entropy. Streaming enumeration limits live memory,
but its runtime grows exponentially. Log-space combinatorics avoids coefficient
overflow and supports much larger distributions. At macroscopic sizes, the
heads fraction concentrates near one half even though the probability of each
individual central macrostate decreases. Rare entropy decreases remain
consistent with the statistical interpretation of the Second Law.
