\begin{center}
  \textbf{Abstract}
\end{center}
\begin{quote}
This practice studies a system of independent, unbiased binary elements using the language of microstates, macrostates, multiplicity and Boltzmann entropy. A Python program enumerates small systems, calculates binomial multiplicities, creates macrostate-distribution plots and evaluates probabilities and entropy differences. We then use analytical approximations to examine the macroscopic limit, $N\sim10^{23}$. The results illustrate why macrostates close to equal numbers of heads and tails dominate the probability distribution and how this statistical behaviour gives rise to the Second Law of Thermodynamics.
\end{quote}

\textbf{Repository:} \href{https://github.com/martinlagunamolina-commits/Laboratory-1-statistical-physics}{GitHub project repository}

% ===================== INTRODUCTION =====================
\section{Introduction}
The system consists of $N$ independent, unbiased coins. Each coin has two possible outcomes, heads (H) or tails (T), and every complete configuration is assumed to be equally probable. A \emph{microstate} specifies the outcome of every coin, while a \emph{macrostate} is specified here by the total number of heads, $k$.

There are $2^N$ possible microstates. The multiplicity of the macrostate with $k$ heads is the number of ways to choose which $k$ coins show heads:
\begin{equation}
  \Omega(N,k)=\binom Nk=\frac{N!}{k!(N-k)!}.
  \label{eq:multiplicity}
\end{equation}
Since all microstates are equiprobable, the probability of a macrostate is
\begin{equation}
  P_k=\frac{\Omega(N,k)}{2^N}.
  \label{eq:probability}
\end{equation}
The Boltzmann entropy associated with a macrostate is
\begin{equation}
  S(k)=k_B\ln\Omega(N,k),
  \label{eq:entropy}
\end{equation}
where $k_B=1.380649\times10^{-23}\ {\rm J\,K^{-1}}$ is Boltzmann's constant.

% ===================== COMPUTATIONAL METHOD =====================
\section{Computational Method}
The project is implemented in Python with separate modules for microstate enumeration, combinatorics, entropy and performance tests. The main program calls these functions, checks the calculated multiplicities, saves numerical results and generates vector PDF figures. For large binomial coefficients, logarithmic calculations are used to avoid integer-to-floating-point overflow:
\begin{equation}
  \ln\Omega(N,k)=\ln\Gamma(N+1)-\ln\Gamma(k+1)-\ln\Gamma(N-k+1).
  \label{eq:log-gamma}
\end{equation}
For distributions over all $k$, a recurrence relation is also used:
\begin{equation}
  \Omega(N,0)=1,\qquad
  \Omega(N,k+1)=\Omega(N,k)\frac{N-k}{k+1}.
  \label{eq:recurrence}
\end{equation}
The recurrence avoids recalculating factorials for every macrostate. Unit tests compare the computational results with exact binomial values for small systems.

\subsection{Extrapolation to the Thermodynamic Limit}
For unbiased coins, the number of heads follows a binomial distribution with mean and standard deviation
\begin{equation}
  \langle k\rangle=\frac{N}{2},\qquad
  \sigma_k=\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}.
  \label{eq:mean-std}
\end{equation}
Near the centre, the binomial distribution is well approximated by a Gaussian:
\begin{equation}
  P_k\simeq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi N}}
  \exp\!\left[-\frac{2(k-N/2)^2}{N}\right].
  \label{eq:gaussian}
\end{equation}
The relative fluctuation is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\sigma_k}{\langle k\rangle}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}
  \longrightarrow 0 \qquad (N\to\infty).
  \label{eq:relative-fluctuation}
\end{equation}
For $N=10^{23}$, the mean is $5.0\times10^{22}$ heads, the standard deviation is $1.5811\times10^{11}$ heads, and the relative fluctuation is $3.1623\times10^{-12}$. Thus, the absolute width grows as $\sqrt{N}$, but its size relative to the system becomes negligible. This is the thermodynamic-limit concentration around equal numbers of heads and tails.

\subsection{Large-\texorpdfstring{$N$}{N} Limit: \texorpdfstring{$N\sim10^{23}$}{N about 10 to the 23}}
We now reconsider parts (a)--(j) for $N=10^{23}$. Direct enumeration is impossible because there are $2^N$ microstates. We instead use analytical approximations. For this even value of $N$, the central macrostate is exactly $k=N/2$.

For a fixed, small number $k$ of heads,
\begin{equation}
  \binom Nk=\frac{N(N-1)\cdots(N-k+1)}{k!}
  \sim\frac{N^k}{k!},\qquad
  P_k\sim\frac{N^k}{k!\,2^N}.
  \label{eq:fixed-k-largeN}
\end{equation}
Consequently, it is more useful to report the logarithm of the probability for exactly 30 heads:
\begin{equation}
  \log_{10}P(k=30)\simeq-3.0103\times10^{22}.
  \label{eq:largeN-p30}
\end{equation}
For the central macrostate, Stirling's approximation gives
\begin{equation}
  P(k=N/2)\simeq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi N}}
  =2.5231\times10^{-12}.
  \label{eq:largeN-central}
\end{equation}
For a fixed integer $m\ll\sqrt{N}$, the $2m+1$ probabilities in the interval $N/2-m\leq k\leq N/2+m$ are practically equal to the central probability. Thus,
\begin{equation}
  P\!\left(\left|k-\frac N2\right|\leq m\right)
  \simeq(2m+1)\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi N}}.
  \label{eq:largeN-window}
\end{equation}
For an offset $m$ from the centre, the entropy difference is approximately
\begin{equation}
  S\!\left(\frac N2+m\right)-S\!\left(\frac N2\right)
  \simeq-\frac{2k_Bm^2}{N}.
  \label{eq:largeN-deltaS}
\end{equation}
For part (g), comparison with the macrostate containing only 20 heads gives, to leading order,
\begin{equation}
  S\!\left(\frac N2\right)-S(20)\simeq Nk_B\ln2.
  \label{eq:largeN-deltaS20}
\end{equation}

For part (h), we again assume one complete $N$-coin trial per second. The expected waiting time is so large that its base-10 logarithm is the most informative representation. For part (i), the likelihood ratio approaches one because a fixed displacement of ten heads is negligible compared with the distribution width when $N$ is macroscopic. More precisely, its logarithm is approximately $2(10)^2/N$.

The natural width of the distribution is
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_k=\frac{\sqrt N}{2}=1.5811\times10^{11}\ \text{heads},\qquad
  \frac{\sigma_k}{N/2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt N}=3.1623\times10^{-12}.
  \label{eq:largeN-fluctuations}
\end{equation}
Therefore, windows of only 10 or 20 heads are extremely narrow compared with the typical fluctuation scale. The central macrostate is the most probable individual macrostate, but its probability tends to zero as $N^{-1/2}$. Most of the total probability is spread over a number of macrostates of order $\sqrt N$.

\subsection{Second Law Interpretation}
None of the results contradicts the Second Law of Thermodynamics. In this model, the macrostate entropy is determined by the number of microstates compatible with that macrostate. A macrostate near $N/2$ has a much larger multiplicity than a strongly ordered macrostate such as $k=20$, and is therefore much more probable. For finite $N$, entropy-decreasing fluctuations are not forbidden: they have a non-zero probability. The Second Law is statistical rather than an absolute prohibition of such fluctuations. As $N$ becomes macroscopic, the probability of strongly ordered macrostates becomes extraordinarily small, while relative fluctuations vanish as $1/\sqrt N$. This explains why macroscopic systems overwhelmingly evolve toward behaviour associated with higher entropy.

% ===================== CONCLUSION =====================
\section{Conclusion}
The coin model shows how a simple set of independent binary elements produces a strongly peaked macrostate distribution. Exact enumeration is practical only for small $N$, whereas multiplicities can be calculated for much larger systems without listing every microstate. For $N\sim10^{23}$, the distribution is concentrated near equal numbers of heads and tails in relative terms, despite having a large absolute fluctuation width. The results give a statistical interpretation of entropy and explain why the Second Law is overwhelmingly reliable for macroscopic systems.
